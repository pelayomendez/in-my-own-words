AI that writes like you, because it's read you.

Point it at your blog. It reads your last few articles and works out how you actually write — your rhythm, your habits, the words you never use — then writes new pieces in your voice, fixes drafts that came out sounding like a robot, and tells you when something doesn't sound like you.

It's a skill: a small add-on you install into the AI assistant you already use. One command, no account, and it works the same in Claude Code, Cursor, Codex and about seventy others.

One email. Written twice.

We asked an AI to write the same thing you've written a hundred times: an email telling your team the launch has slipped. First as it comes out of the box. Then with a profile loaded.

The profile belongs to David Ogilvy, who more or less invented modern advertising copy in the 1950s and spent forty years insisting that most business writing is bad on purpose — padded, hedged, and written to protect the writer rather than inform the reader. He's the demo because he's the opposite of how a machine writes by default, so if the profile works at all, you'll see it immediately.

Straight out of the box

I hope this email finds you well. I wanted to reach out and touch base regarding the Q3 launch timeline. Following recent conversations with key stakeholders, it's important to note that we will be shifting the delivery date to November 14th.

We appreciate your flexibility as we navigate these changes, and we remain confident that this adjustment will ultimately allow us to deliver a stronger product. Please don't hesitate to reach out with any questions.

Same request, with the Ogilvy profile loaded

The launch moves to 14 November.

That is three weeks late, and it is my fault. I approved a scope in June that two of you told me at the time was too big.

Nothing else changes. Same team, same budget, same feature list. We are buying the three weeks, not spending them.

If you think 14 November is wrong too, tell me this week. Not in November.

Left: what the profile says about him. Right: what it did.
  • "Assertion first, evidence second, never the reverse."The news is the first six words. The plain version buries it in the middle of the second sentence, behind three clauses of warm-up.
  • "Confess a failure. This is what buys the authority for everything else.""It is my fault." No AI volunteers that unless the profile tells it this writer does.
  • "Specific over general, always. A number, a year, a client. Adjectives are treated as a failure to find a fact.""Three weeks late." "A scope I approved in June." The plain version manages zero facts in ninety words.
  • "The imperative close. Never a summary, never a softening.""Tell me this week. Not in November." Against a plain version that ends by inviting questions it clearly doesn't want.
  • Never writes: "reach out", "stakeholders", "it's important to note", "navigate", "ultimately", "don't hesitate".Seven hits in the plain version, marked in red. Knowing what a writer never says turns out to do more work than any instruction about what they do.

A style demonstration, not something Ogilvy wrote — he died in 1999 and never had a Q3 launch. Your profile would be built from your own articles, on your own computer, and the emails would sound like you instead.

Why telling an AI to "write like me" never works

Ask any writer to describe their own style and you get the same three words: clear, conversational, a bit witty. It's what we'd all like to be true. It tells an AI nothing.

So this goes the other way round. Instead of asking what you sound like, it reads what you've published and writes down what you actually do — how long your paragraphs run, how your sentences start, which word you put in italics, how you end a piece. Every single thing it notices comes with a quote from your own writing, so you can look at it and say: no, that's not me, that was one article.

The most useful part turned out to be the opposite list. Knowing that you have never once typed "it's important to note" keeps a draft sounding like you better than any instruction about what to write.

And then it tells you you're wrong

It also interviews you — but never with "how would you describe your writing?" It puts two versions of your own sentence side by side and asks which is more you. Eight of those, plus a couple of questions the text can't answer: who's the reader in your head, which of your own pieces would you take down, what do you always cut at the end.

Then it lays your answers next to your actual writing and shows you where the two disagree. Your answers don't win those arguments — the text does. What you say describes the writer you mean to be, and writing to that is exactly the failure this thing exists to prevent.

It's also the most interesting thing anyone will tell you about your own writing this year.

The conflict report

You said you keep things short.
Your last five pieces average 1,400 words. The shortest is 1,100.

You said you always cut adverbs in the final pass.
There are 31 in the corpus. Nine of them are in the piece you named as your best.

You picked the version without the conjunction opening six times out of eight.
You start 40% of your paragraphs with "And" or "But".

Five things it does

All five work off the same profile, so what it writes and what it corrects can never contradict each other.

Write

From notes to finished piece

Give it your ideas, an outline, or a mess of bullet points. It keeps your ideas in the order you gave them — quietly rearranging your argument into something more conventional is the most common way this goes wrong.

Rewrite

Fix a draft that doesn't sound like you

Yours, a colleague's, or something an AI produced. Same points, same order, same facts. It changes how it's written and tells you what it touched.

Check

Point at what's off

It reads a piece and quotes the exact sentences that stopped sounding like you, and says why. It won't rewrite them — that part stays yours.

Grill

It interviews you

Not "describe your style" — it shows you two versions of your own sentence and asks which is more you. Then it tells you where your answers and your writing disagree.

Keep up

It learns as you keep writing

Show it something new you've published. It picks up only what's changed, shows you before saving anything, and keeps a note of what it learned and where from.

What it actually learns about you

Most attempts at this stop at "short sentences, friendly tone", which steers nothing. This is the level of detail it takes before an AI can genuinely sound like someone — and here's a piece of the Ogilvy profile so you can see it.

Because every line comes from something the writer actually wrote, you can argue with it. A profile is a claim about you with the evidence attached.

A page from the Ogilvy profile

Rhythm

Short sentences. Subject, verb, object. Compound sentences are rare and complex ones rarer.

Paragraphs of 40–80 words, three or four sentences, almost never one. But the single-sentence paragraph is used as a hammer — reserved for the line he wants you to remember, roughly once per section, always at the end of a run of evidence.

It writes in your other language too — and says when it can't

Most of what makes your writing yours isn't vocabulary. It's how long you let a paragraph run, how you build up to a point, how you land an ending. That part travels between languages, so it does too.

Your actual words don't travel, so they're kept separately, one set per language. Ask for something in a language it has never seen you write in and it will use the part that travels, then tell you plainly that it's guessing at the rest. It won't invent a habit you don't have.

Travels between languages
  • How long your paragraphs run
  • How you build an argument
  • Whether you reach for metaphors, and how often
  • How you open, and how you end
  • Whether you argue from evidence or from experience
Doesn't — kept per language
  • The words and turns of phrase you reach for
  • Punctuation habits
  • How formal or colloquial you are
  • Whether you speak to the reader as "you"
  • The phrases you never use

Install

One command. No account, no API key, nothing to configure. Your writing never leaves your computer.

$npx skills@latest add pelayomendez/in-my-own-words -g -a claude-code

The -g makes it available everywhere. Drop it to install for one project only.

Or as a Claude Code plugin

>/plugin marketplace add pelayomendez/in-my-own-words

Then, three steps

Show it your writing

A link to your blog, or three to five pieces. Fewer than three and it's describing one article, not you.

learn my writing voice from https://your-blog.com/feed

Tell it where it's wrong

It shows you everything it thinks it found before saving any of it.

that's not a habit, that was one article

Write

A new piece, a fix for an old one, or a second opinion on a draft.

does this sound like me?

Stop describing your voice. Show it the receipts.

Free, open source, and nothing gets uploaded anywhere. If you fork it to keep your own profile backed up, make the fork private — a voice profile is a fairly complete recipe for impersonating you.
