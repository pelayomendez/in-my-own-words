Every writer describes their own style the same way.

Clear. Conversational. A bit witty. It steers nothing, which is why style prompts don't work. This skill reads what you've actually published and records what you demonstrably do — then drafts, rewrites and audits against it.

The same paragraph, twice

A model was asked to argue that long advertising copy outsells short copy. Then the same request went through a profile built from David Ogilvy's writing. Nothing about the argument changed.

Model default

In today's fast-paced marketing landscape, it's important to note that long-form copy can be a game-changer. Studies have shown that longer advertisements often outperform shorter ones. By leveraging detailed, informative content, brands can unlock deeper engagement with their audience.

Ultimately, the key is finding the right balance for your specific situation.

Same request, profile applied

Long copy sells more than short copy.

I have tested this against my own taste and lost. Every time we cut an advertisement down to something a creative director would admire, it sold less.

Nobody reads long copy, they told me. The sales figures disagreed.

Write the long one.

Traced to the profile
  • Assertion first, evidence second, never the reverse.The claim is the first sentence. The "before" version buries it behind two clauses of scene-setting.
  • Confess a failure. This is what buys the authority for everything else."I have tested this against my own taste and lost." No model volunteers this unless the profile says it's a habit.
  • Attack the fashionable position by name."Nobody reads long copy, they told me."
  • The imperative close. Never a summary, never a softening."Write the long one." — against the default's hedge that gives the reader both options and commits to neither.
  • Anti-tells: "leverage", "unlock", "game-changer", "it's important to note", "in today's fast-paced world".Five hits in the default, marked in red above. Recording what an author never writes does more work than any positive instruction.

Ogilvy is the worked example because his prose is unusually well documented and he has been dead since 1999. Your profile is built from your own corpus, on your machine.

Four things it does

All four run against the same profile, so an audit and a draft can't disagree about what you sound like.

Draft

From notes to finished piece

Give it ideas, an outline or a mess. Your argument order is treated as the spec — reordering it into a more conventional shape is the single most common way this goes wrong, so it doesn't.

Rewrite

Your draft, your voice

Same claims, same order, same evidence. It changes the prose and reports what it touched, tied to named traits rather than to taste.

Audit

Where it stops sounding like you

Fifteen dimensions, a verdict each, always with the offending sentence quoted. It won't rewrite. Factual claims are enumerated separately as unverified rather than passed or failed.

Refresh

A profile that tracks you

Point it at new writing. It extracts only what's new or contradictory, shows you the diff, and logs what it learned. Profiles accumulate; they never get replaced from one source.

The hard part isn't the mechanics

It's knowing how specific a profile has to get before it does any work. Most attempts stop at "short sentences, friendly tone" and steer nothing. This is roughly the altitude required — every line traceable to something the author actually wrote.

Which is also why you can argue with it. A profile is a claim about you, with evidence attached, and you get to tell it it's wrong.

## Rhythm

Short sentences. Subject, verb, object. Compound sentences are rare and complex ones rarer.

Paragraphs of 40–80 words, three or four sentences, almost never one. But the single-sentence paragraph is used as a hammer — reserved for the line he wants you to remember, roughly once per section, always at the end of a run of evidence.

It crosses languages without pretending

The profile is two layers. core-voice.md holds what survives translation — shape, rhythm, argument moves, how a piece lands. Almost none of it is vocabulary. lexicon.<lang>.md holds what doesn't.

Ask for a piece in a language whose lexicon is still empty and it applies the core, derives only what safely follows from an observed habit, and says so on delivery. It will not invent verbal tics in a language it has never seen you write. A fabricated habit is worse than a neutral sentence.

Install

One command. No API key, no account, no build step. Your writing never leaves your machine.

$npx skills@latest add pelayomendez/sounds-like-me -g -a claude-code

-g installs globally, into your agent's user directory. Drop it for this project only.

Or as a Claude Code plugin

>/plugin marketplace add pelayomendez/sounds-like-me

Then, three steps

Point it at your writing

Three to five published pieces, or a feed. Below three you're documenting one article, not a voice.

bootstrap my voice profile from https://your-blog.com/feed

Argue with the profile

It shows you what it found, with quotations, before saving anything. Tell it where it's wrong.

that's not a habit, that was one article

Write

Draft, rewrite or audit. In any of your languages.

does this sound like me?

Stop describing your voice. Show it the receipts.

MIT, no telemetry, nothing uploaded. Fork it, build your own profile, keep the fork private — a voice profile is a fairly complete recipe for impersonating you.
