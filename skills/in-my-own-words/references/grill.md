# The grill

An interview that doesn't ask people to describe their own writing.

## Why it exists at all

The corpus is the better witness and it always wins. But there are things it cannot tell you, and guessing at them is where profiles go wrong:

- **Who you're writing for.** Two pieces with identical mechanics can be aimed at completely different readers.
- **Which of your own pieces you disown.** A published article you now think is bad is still in the feed, still being counted as evidence.
- **What you cut.** The corpus shows what survived the last edit. It cannot show what you removed every single time, which is often the sharpest signal you have.
- **What's deliberate versus what's habit.** Both look identical on the page. Only one should be reinforced.
- **Where you're going.** A voice in motion looks like an inconsistent voice from the outside.

So: interview for those. Never for the mechanics — those come from the text.

## The rules

1. **Never ask "how would you describe your writing?"** It is the question this whole skill exists because of. Every writer answers it the same way and the answer steers nothing.
2. **Forced choice over open questions.** People cannot describe their style, but they recognise it instantly next to an alternative. Show two options and make them pick.
3. **Use their own text.** Take real sentences from the corpus and produce a variant. The variant should differ in exactly one dimension — length, sentence opening, punctuation, hedging — so the answer tells you something specific.
4. **One question at a time.** A wall of eight questions gets three lazy answers.
5. **Eight to twelve questions.** Past that people start performing rather than answering.
6. **Ask why only when they surprise you.** Following up on every answer turns it into an interrogation.
7. **Never argue with an answer during the interview.** Record it. The contradictions get presented at the end, all together, as a report.

## Running it

Read the corpus first. You cannot build forced-choice questions without real sentences, and an interview run before the corpus is just the bad question in disguise.

### Opening — orientation (2 questions, open)

- Who is the reader in your head when you write? Not the demographic — one person, if there is one.
- Which of these pieces would you take down if you could? *(list their own titles)* What's wrong with it?

The second one is the most useful question in the set. It reclassifies evidence.

### Middle — forced choice (6 to 8)

Build these from their actual text. Each isolates one dimension:

> **Which is more you?**
> A) *(their real sentence)*
> B) *(same content, one dimension changed)*

Dimensions worth testing, one per question: sentence opening (conjunction vs. not) · paragraph length · a hedge kept vs. cut · a metaphor vs. a plain statement · a short closing line vs. a summarising one · direct address to the reader vs. impersonal · a concrete number vs. a general claim · an idiom vs. its neutral equivalent.

Mix in two of these, which test recognition rather than preference:

> **Which of these three openings did you write?** *(one theirs, two decoys in the same register)*
>
> **You wrote this line. Did you mean it to land as [X] or as [Y]?**

### Closing — the two that pay off (2)

- What do you always cut in the final pass?
- Is there something you do that you wish you didn't?

Both surface habits the author already has an opinion about. The second one is the only place aspiration is allowed anywhere in this skill, and it's allowed because it's negative — a thing to stop doing is safe to record. A thing to start doing is not; see below.

## Where the answers go

Everything from the grill goes to `stated.md`, never directly into `core-voice.md` or a lexicon. Each entry is dated and carries the question that produced it.

**Precedence, when the two disagree: the corpus wins.** Stated preferences describe intent; the corpus describes practice. Writing to someone's intent produces text that sounds like the writer they mean to be, which is the failure this skill exists to prevent.

The exception is scope and audience, which the corpus cannot observe and where the stated answer is the only evidence there is.

## The conflict report

This is the reason to run a grill at all. At the end, present every place the answers and the corpus disagree, together, with evidence on both sides:

> You said you keep things short. Your last five pieces average 1,400 words, and the shortest is 1,100.
>
> You said you always cut adverbs in the final pass. There are 31 in the corpus, 9 of them in the piece you named as your best.
>
> You picked the version without the conjunction opening in 6 of 8 questions. You open with "And" or "But" in 40% of your paragraphs.

Do not resolve these. Present them and ask. Each one has three possible readings and only the author knows which:

- **The corpus is stale** — this is a habit they've already dropped. Record the shift with a date; weight recent sources higher.
- **The stated answer is aspiration** — they want it to be true and it isn't. Record it in `stated.md` as an intent, marked as such, and leave the profile alone.
- **Genre split** — they do keep it short somewhere the corpus doesn't cover. Ask for a sample of that, and scope the trait.

A grill that produces no conflicts was a badly built grill. Go back and make the forced choices harder.

## When the answers say the corpus is wrong

Sometimes an author rejects a sentence that came verbatim out of their own published work. The reflex is to file it as aspiration — they'd like to be more restrained than they are. Check something first.

**Ask who wrote it.** If the source is `assisted` or `drafted`, they may be right and the corpus wrong: what they're rejecting is the model's habit, published under their name. They are not describing who they want to be; they are correcting the record.

Two things separate the cases, and you cannot tell them apart from the interview alone:

- **A direction.** Decontamination has one — the rejected traits are all model tics, and they all fail in the same way. Aspiration is scattered.
- **A clean source.** Something written before the model, or without it. If the author's forced choices match their hand-written work, the corpus was contaminated. If they don't, it was aspiration. Ask for that source; it is the only thing that settles it.

When it is contamination, the rejected traits move to the lexicon's **anti-tells** rather than being deleted, and the affected sources get their provenance recorded so the next refresh weights them properly.

Beware the reverse error too. "A model helped me" is also a comfortable explanation for a habit someone simply doesn't like about themselves. If a trait shows up in hand-written sources as well, it is theirs, and the honest answer is to say so.

## What is deliberately not collected

**Writing by other people that the author admires.** It is tempting — it's easy to gather and people enjoy talking about it — and it would quietly wreck the profile. Admired writing is a description of who someone would like to sound like. Feed it in and the output drifts toward that writer, the author can't tell you why the drafts feel slightly off, and the one thing this tool promises stops being true.

If the author volunteers it, say what it is: useful information about their taste, not evidence about their voice. It does not go in `stated.md` and it does not go in the profile.

## Re-running

Worth repeating roughly once a year, or after any stretch where the writing has visibly changed. Keep the old answers with their dates rather than overwriting them — the drift between two grills two years apart is itself a finding, and it's one nothing else in this skill can see.
