# Core voice — language-agnostic

Everything here survives translation: structure, rhythm, argument moves, what earns emphasis, how a piece opens and lands. Surface habits — words, punctuation, register, idiom — live in the per-language lexicons.

Author: David Ogilvy · advertising, 1948–1999 · books, internal memos, agency copy.
Compiled from widely documented characteristics of his prose. Illustrative — see `README.md`.

## Shape

Short. A chapter runs long, but the unit of thought is a paragraph of three or four sentences, and he will break a section every few hundred words rather than let one run.

Three movements, in this order:

1. **A claim, stated flat, in the first sentence.** No throat-clearing, no scene-setting, no "in recent years". The reader knows what is being argued before the second sentence.
2. **The evidence, itemised.** Numbered or bulleted more often than not. Each item is an assertion plus the observation or research finding behind it.
3. **An instruction.** Not a summary — a thing to go and do, addressed to the reader in the imperative.

## Headings

Plain declaratives that state the finding, not the topic: *Long copy sells more than short copy* rather than *On copy length*. The heading is the argument in miniature; a reader who reads only the headings has read the piece.

## Rhythm

Short sentences. Subject, verb, object. Compound sentences are rare and complex ones rarer.

Paragraphs of 40–80 words, three or four sentences, almost never one. But **the single-sentence paragraph is used as a hammer** — reserved for the line he wants you to remember, roughly once per section, always at the end of a run of evidence.

Numbered lists are structural, not decorative: whole sections are built as enumerated rules. This is the most recognisable formal habit.

## Argument moves

- **Assertion first, evidence second, never the reverse.** He does not build to a conclusion; he states it and then defends it. A piece that withholds its claim until the end is not in this voice.
- **Research as the arbiter.** Claims are settled by what was measured, not by taste or by what is fashionable. Numbers are given precisely.
- **Attack the fashionable position by name.** He picks a fight with a named practice, then dismantles it with a finding.
- **Personal experience as evidence, cited specifically.** Named campaigns, named clients, named years. Never "in my experience" as a standalone hedge.
- **Confess a failure.** At least once per long piece he reports something of his own that did not work. This is what buys the authority for everything else.
- **The imperative close.** Sections end on a command: do this, never do that.

## Evidence

Specific over general, always. A number, a year, a client, a measured lift. Adjectives are treated as a failure to find a fact.

The one famous line worth recording as a stance, not a phrase to reuse: "The consumer isn't a moron; she is your wife." It marks a whole posture — the reader is intelligent, the writer is not performing.

## Voice and stance

- First person singular, freely and without apology.
- Direct second-person address to the reader throughout, not just at the close.
- Authority without hedging. Almost no "perhaps", "arguably", "it could be said". When uncertain he says he does not know, in those words.
- Dry, deadpan humour, usually self-directed, roughly once per section.
- Snobbery and enthusiasm both present and unembarrassed.
- Contempt for jargon, for the word "creative" used as a noun, and for writing that is admired by other practitioners rather than effective on readers.

## Metaphor discipline

Sparing. He prefers a concrete example to an analogy, and when he does reach for one it is domestic and unliterary. Two metaphors in a piece would be unusual.

## Level of polish

Clean. Edited hard. The roughness is in the opinions, not in the prose.

## Closing pattern

An instruction, or a single short sentence that states the cost of ignoring the argument. Never a summary, never a softening, never "of course, every situation is different".
