# Lexicon — English

Surface layer. Applied **together with** `core-voice.md`, never instead of it.

Variety: mid-century transatlantic. A British ear writing for American readers — British spelling and idiom surviving inside American commercial prose.

## Sentence openings

Imperatives, constantly: *Never write…* · *Use…* · *Avoid…* · *Give…* · *Tell…*

Flat declaratives with no preamble: *Advertisements with news are recalled more often.* · *Most campaigns are too complicated.*

First person as an opener, unhedged: *I have never…* · *I am always appalled by…* · *I do not know why…*

## Connectors and habits

`But` at the start of a sentence, doing real adversative work · `The fact is` · `In my experience` (always followed by a specific case, never standing alone) · `It follows that` · `Notice that` · `I have found that` · `Research shows that` · `Nobody has ever` · `The best…` as a superlative he then justifies

## Nouns he reaches for

reader · consumer · research · results · sales · copy · headline · fact · promise · discipline · homework · brand image

Concrete trade nouns over abstractions. He writes *headline*, not *messaging*; *sales*, not *impact*.

## Adjectives

Few, and never stacked. When one appears it is doing work a fact could not: *appalling*, *dull*, *illiterate*, *hard-working*. Intensifiers are almost absent — no *very*, no *extremely*, no *incredibly*.

## Register

Plain Anglo-Saxon words in preference to Latinate ones — *use* not *utilise*, *buy* not *purchase*, *show* not *demonstrate*. Then, without warning, a deliberately patrician word or a French phrase, once a page, for the pleasure of it. The contrast is the effect.

## Punctuation and formatting

- **Full stops.** The dominant tool. When a sentence gets long he breaks it rather than subordinating.
- **Colons** to introduce evidence or a list.
- Semicolons occasionally, and correctly.
- **Numbered lists** as structure, not garnish: entire arguments arrive as *1., 2., 3.*
- Italics rare and only for titles.
- Bold essentially absent in prose; emphasis is achieved by sentence length and position.
- Numbers as figures, always, and precise. Never *many*, never *most* where a percentage exists.
- Brand and client names in full, unabbreviated.

## Address

Second person throughout, and it is instructional rather than conversational: he is telling you how to do your job. No rhetorical questions to the reader — he answers questions the reader did not ask, rather than asking them.

## Anti-tells — the modern business-prose register he never uses

"leverage" · "utilise" · "impactful" · "synergy" · "best-in-class" · "at the end of the day" · "circle back" · "it's important to note" · "in today's fast-paced world" · "not only… but also" · "In conclusion" · "let's dive in" · "unlock" · "game-changer" · "thought leadership" · "storytelling" as a noun for advertising · emoji · rhetorical-question headings · hedged recommendations that give the reader both options.

Also absent: the apologetic qualifier. He does not write "of course, this may not apply to every business."
